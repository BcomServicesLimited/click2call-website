SIP Trunks

What Is a SIP Trunk? A Plain-English Guide for Australian Businesses

A SIP trunk is a phone line delivered over the internet that connects a business PBX to the phone network. How it works, channels, costs and SIP vs cloud phone systems in Australia.

Reviewed by Royce Clark, Director · Last checked 26 November 2026

By Royce Clark • Updated 26 Nov 2026

A SIP trunk is a phone line delivered over the internet. It connects a business’s own phone system (a PBX) to the public telephone network using SIP, the Session Initiation Protocol, so the business can make and receive ordinary phone calls on its numbers without copper phone lines. In Australia, SIP trunks have replaced ISDN and analogue business lines as the NBN has replaced the copper network.

SIP trunks at a glance

What it is:
a virtual phone line between your PBX and the phone network, carried over your internet connection.
What it replaces:
ISDN and analogue (PSTN) business phone lines.
What you need:
a SIP-capable PBX (for example 3CX, FreePBX or Asterisk) and a reliable internet connection.
Capacity:
measured in channels: one channel carries one call at a time.
Who it suits:
businesses that already own a PBX. Without one, a hosted cloud phone system is usually simpler.

What does SIP stand for?

SIP stands for Session Initiation Protocol, an open standard published by the Internet Engineering Task Force as RFC 3261 in 2002. SIP sets up, changes and ends calls between devices: it is the “signalling” that says who is calling whom. The voice itself travels separately, in a stream called RTP (Real-time Transport Protocol). A trunk is the telecoms word for a line that carries many calls between two systems, rather than one phone.

How does a SIP trunk work?

Your PBX connects over the internet to your provider’s network, usually to a session border controller (SBC) that protects and routes calls. The provider connects the call onwards to the public telephone network, so you can call any landline or mobile, and anyone can call your numbers.

Each call uses a voice codec, the method that turns speech into data. Common codecs are G.711 (standard phone quality, about 64 kbps of audio), G.722 (“HD voice”) and G.729 (compressed, for slow connections). To keep calls private, the call setup can be encrypted with TLS and the voice with SRTP (Secure RTP).

What is a SIP channel, and how many do I need?

A channel is one call in progress, in or out. Ten channels let a business have ten calls at the same time; an eleventh caller hears busy or goes to voicemail. ISDN sold capacity in fixed blocks (an ISDN 10 had ten channels, for example). A SIP trunk’s capacity is set in software, so it can change without new cabling.

A common rule of thumb is one channel for every three to four staff who share the phones, because not everyone is on a call at once. Sales teams and call centres need more; a quiet office needs fewer. If callers start hearing busy tones while someone is free, you are short of channels.

What do I need to use a SIP trunk?

  • A SIP-capable PBX. Most modern systems support SIP, including 3CX, FreePBX and Asterisk.
  • A reliable internet connection. Each call needs roughly 30–100 kbps in each direction depending on the codec, but low jitter and a stable connection matter more than raw speed.
  • A way to connect. Most PBXs register with a username and password. Larger installations can use IP peering, where the provider and the PBX trust each other’s fixed IP address instead of a login.
  • A router that doesn’t interfere. A router feature called SIP ALG often causes one-way audio and dropped registrations; using TLS avoids it. Never forward SIP ports (5060/5061) to a PBX from the internet: it invites fraudulent calls.

Can I keep my phone numbers on a SIP trunk?

Yes. Australian local, 1300 and 1800 numbers can be ported to a new provider and delivered over the trunk, and so can most mobile numbers (eligibility depends on the current carrier). Each number arrives as a direct-dial number (DDI), so the PBX can route it to the right extension, queue or menu. At Click2Call a port costs $100 including GST per number and usually takes 5 to 10 business days; the number keeps working with the old provider until the switchover. See how number porting works.

Is a SIP trunk reliable?

On a stable business internet connection, call quality matches a traditional line, and continuity can be better: if the office loses power or internet, the provider can send calls to mobiles or another site automatically. The trade-off is that calls depend on the internet connection and on power to the PBX and router, so it pays to plan for outages. Read calls during a power or internet outage.

Do I need a SIP trunk at all?

Only if you want to keep your own PBX. If you don’t have one, or it’s due for replacement, a cloud phone system gives the same calling with no hardware to maintain: each person gets a Business Line on their phone, computer or desk phone, and menus, queues and recording are managed in a web portal.

Key terms

SIP (Session Initiation Protocol)
The open standard (RFC 3261) that sets up, changes and ends calls over the internet.
SIP trunk
A virtual phone line carrying many calls between a business PBX and a provider, over the internet.
Channel
One call in progress. Channels set how many calls can happen at once.
PBX (private branch exchange)
A business’s own phone system: extensions, transfers, menus and voicemail.
DDI (direct dial-in)
A phone number delivered over a trunk to a specific extension or service.
SBC (session border controller)
The provider’s gateway that secures and routes SIP calls.
RTP / SRTP
The stream that carries the voice; SRTP is the encrypted version.
Codec
The method that turns speech into data, such as G.711, G.722 or G.729.
